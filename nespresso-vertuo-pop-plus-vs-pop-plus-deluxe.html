Nespresso Vertuo Pop+ vs. Pop+ Deluxe: What $60 and a Bigger Tank Buys

Both machines run the same Vertuo capsule system and the same one-touch brewing, read by the same barcode on the rim of every pod. The Deluxe costs $60 more and its listing states a larger 37-ounce side tank and automatic capsule ejection, against the standard Pop+'s 25-ounce tank. Here's what that $60 is actually paying for.

The short answer

If you live alone or make one or two cups a day, buy the standard Nespresso Vertuo Pop+ at $89.00 and keep the $60. Its 25-ounce side tank is enough water for roughly six to eight Vertuo pods before a refill, which for a single-person routine means refilling every couple of days rather than every morning.

If more than one person in the house drinks from this machine, or you just don't want to think about the tank, the Nespresso Vertuo Pop+ Deluxe at $99.00 states a 37-ounce side tank — roughly 48% more capacity — plus automatic capsule ejection, for $60 more. That is not a trivial markup on an $89 machine, but it buys a real reduction in how often you touch the tank, which is the single most repetitive task on a capsule machine.

Both machines brew from the exact same Vertuo capsule line, read the exact same barcode, and cost the exact same per cup. The entire decision is the tank and the eject mechanism — nothing else in either listing changes.

Same capsule system, same button

Before getting into what's different, it's worth being precise about what isn't. Both the Pop+ and the Pop+ Deluxe are Vertuo machines, De'Longhi-built, sold under the Nespresso Vertuo line. Vertuo capsules carry a barcode printed around the rim, and the machine reads that barcode to set brew volume and spin speed automatically — you don't choose a cup size or a shot length yourself, the pod tells the machine what to do.

That's the "one-touch brewing" both listings advertise: drop in a capsule, close the lever, press the single button on top, and the machine handles the rest. Neither listing states a manual override for volume or strength, and neither machine takes any other capsule format — no Nespresso Original-line capsules, no third-party pods designed for a different reader. Whatever capsule range you're committing to, it's identical between these two machines.

Because the brewing mechanism is shared, the two machines also taste identical, cup for cup, on the same capsule. There is no "better brewing" tier here the way there sometimes is between a base model and a step-up model in other product lines. The Deluxe is not a stronger or more capable brewer — it is the same brewer in a shell with a bigger tank attached.

The tank: 25 oz vs. 37 oz

The standard Pop+ carries a 25-ounce side water tank. The Pop+ Deluxe listing states a 37-ounce side tank — about 12 ounces, or roughly 48%, more water held at once. Both tanks sit on the side of the machine, which is the Vertuo Pop+ body style; neither model uses a rear-mounted tank.

A single Vertuo pod uses somewhere in the neighborhood of 4 to 14 ounces of water depending on the capsule's programmed volume — a small espresso pulls less, a large Alto or carafe-style pod pulls more. We don't have a per-capsule water-draw spec from either listing, so treat that range as a general Vertuo-system fact rather than a number tied to a specific pod. Using it loosely: a 25-ounce tank holds somewhere around 2 to 6 small-format pods' worth of water before it runs dry, and a 37-ounce tank scales that up by the same roughly 48%.

Neither listing states the footprint difference between the two tanks, if any — De'Longhi may fit a taller or deeper tank into the same body shell, or the Deluxe's housing may be slightly larger to accommodate it. If counter depth is tight, check the current listing's dimensions rather than assuming the two machines are interchangeable in footprint.

Why a bigger side tank matters more for pods than for beans

Tank size reads differently on a capsule machine than it does on a bean-to-cup or semi-automatic one, and it's worth spelling out why, because the instinct from other machine categories can mislead here.

On a bean-based machine, the tank mostly sets how long you go between refills on a fixed daily habit — a 60-ounce tank versus a 40-ounce tank buys you extra days before you're back at the sink, but the refill itself is a non-event: lift the tank, run it under a tap, drop it back. The grinder, the boiler and the steam wand are where the real differences between machines live, and the tank is a convenience line item near the bottom of the spec sheet.

On a Vertuo machine, the tank is closer to the whole story. There's no grinder, no boiler to speak of beyond a fast on-demand heater, and no steam wand on either of these two models. Water capacity is one of the only hardware specs that actually separates two machines running an identical capsule system — which is exactly the situation here. When the brewing mechanism is fixed by the capsule format, the tank becomes one of the few remaining levers a manufacturer has to differentiate a "plus" model from a base one, and Nespresso has pulled that lever rather than, say, changing the pump or the heater.

There's also a household-size effect that's sharper on a capsule machine than a bean one. A bean machine's grind-and-brew cycle takes roughly the same couple of minutes regardless of how many people are waiting; a Vertuo brew cycle is already fast, so in a two- or three-person household the bottleneck shifts from "how long does one cup take" to "how many cups can I pull before I have to stop and refill." A 37-ounce tank pushes that refill point meaningfully further out than a 25-ounce one, in a way that matters more here than the same percentage increase would on, say, the 67-ounce tank of a semi-automatic bean machine.

Automatic capsule ejection

The second stated difference is automatic capsule ejection on the Deluxe. Vertuo capsules are read and brewed with the lid closed, and when the cycle finishes, the used capsule has to come out before the next one goes in. On a Vertuo machine without this feature, that's typically a manual step — open the lever, the spent capsule drops or is tipped into an internal bin or straight into the trash, depending on the model's design.

Automatic ejection removes that manual handling: the Deluxe's listing states the used capsule is ejected on its own as part of finishing the cycle, rather than you having to flip it out by hand. Neither listing specifies exactly where the ejected capsule lands — an internal used-capsule bin with its own capacity, or directly into a bin you position underneath — so if that detail matters to your counter setup, check the current listing or product images before buying.

It's a small mechanical step, and on its own it probably wouldn't justify $60. Paired with the bigger tank, though, it reinforces the Deluxe's actual pitch: both of its upgrades are about reducing how often you have to physically interact with the machine between cups, not about brewing a different drink.

The picks

Two machines, one capsule system, so the "picks" here are really one call each way rather than a ranked list.

Pick the standard Pop+ if: you're brewing for one or two people with a normal daily habit, you don't mind lifting a side tank every couple of days, and you'd rather put $60 toward capsules than toward convenience you may not feel. At $89.00 it's also the lower-risk way to find out whether the Vertuo system — and capsule coffee generally — fits how you actually drink coffee, before spending more on the same core machine.

Pick the Pop+ Deluxe if: more than one person pulls from this machine, mornings are rushed enough that fewer tank refills and no manual capsule handling genuinely save you friction, or you simply prefer the matte dark chrome finish the Deluxe's listing describes. At $99.00 you're paying $60 for a tank that's about 48% bigger and one fewer manual step per cup — reasonable if either of those lands for your routine, unnecessary if it doesn't.

Neither machine is the "better" one in an absolute sense. They brew the same coffee from the same capsules at the same speed. The only question worth asking is how often you want to touch the water tank and the used capsule.

Capsule cost is identical either way

It's worth stating plainly, because it's easy to assume a pricier machine comes with a pricier (or cheaper) running cost: it doesn't, here. Both machines are built around the same Vertuo capsule line, sold at the same price per pod regardless of which machine brews them. Buying the Deluxe doesn't unlock a discount on capsules, and buying the standard Pop+ doesn't lock you into a cheaper or more limited pod selection.

The entire cost difference between owning one machine versus the other is the $60 at checkout. Everything downstream of that — the capsules you'll be buying for as long as you own either machine — is the same line item on your grocery bill no matter which one is sitting on your counter. If you're trying to budget the real cost of owning a Vertuo machine, model the capsule spend once and apply it to either machine; the hardware price is the only variable.

Who should save the $60 and stick with the standard Pop+

Three situations where the extra $60 is money spent on convenience you're unlikely to use:

You're the only person using it. A 25-ounce tank refilled every couple of days is a minor chore for a single-person habit, and the automatic ejection saves a motion that takes a couple of seconds either way.

You're testing the format, not committing to it. If this is your first Vertuo machine and you're not sure capsule coffee is going to stick as a habit, the standard Pop+ gets you the identical cup for $60 less. If you end up loving it and resenting the tank, that's a specific, well-understood reason to upgrade later — better than guessing up front.

The finish doesn't matter to you. The Deluxe's listing describes a black matte dark chrome look that's genuinely different from the standard black Pop+. If you don't care about that distinction and you're indifferent to the tank size, there's no functional reason to pay more.

Conversely, don't talk yourself out of the Deluxe purely to save money if you know your household drinks more than a cup or two a day from this machine — at that volume, the standard tank's refill frequency becomes a daily annoyance rather than an occasional one, and $60 is a small price against months of that friction.

Frequently asked questions

Do the Pop+ and Pop+ Deluxe use different capsules?

No. Both machines run on the same Nespresso Vertuo capsule line, read by the same barcode system, at the same price per capsule. The $60 price difference between the machines has no effect on what you pay per cup afterward.

Is the Pop+ Deluxe's larger tank actually worth $60?

It depends on how many people use the machine and how often. For one or two cups a day from a single person, a 25-ounce tank refilled every couple of days is a minor task, and $60 buys more convenience than most single users will notice. For a household pulling several cups a day, the Deluxe's 37-ounce tank — about 48% more capacity — meaningfully cuts how often anyone has to stop and refill, which is where the upgrade earns its price.

What does automatic capsule ejection actually do?

On the Deluxe, the used capsule is ejected on its own once a brew cycle finishes, rather than requiring you to open the lever and tip or flip it out by hand. It's a small convenience on its own — saving a couple of seconds and a bit of handling per cup — that mainly matters paired with the larger tank, since both upgrades reduce how often you touch the machine between cups.

Does the bigger tank change how the coffee tastes?

No. Both machines brew from the identical Vertuo capsule system with the same barcode-reading, one-touch mechanism, so a given capsule tastes the same out of either machine. The tank only affects how long you go between refills, not what happens during the brew itself.
