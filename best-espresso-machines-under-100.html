Steam vs pump: how to tell them apart

Real espresso is brewed by forcing water through compressed, finely-ground coffee at roughly 9 bar of pressure — a fact of the method, not a marketing number, and one our guide to 9 bar covers in more depth. Getting anywhere near that pressure requires an electric pump, which is why every genuine espresso machine, cheap or expensive, is built around one.

A steam-driven machine skips the pump entirely. It heats water in a small boiler until steam pressure alone pushes it through the grounds, and steam pressure in a home appliance tops out far below 9 bar — enough to force water through coffee, not enough to do it the way an espresso machine does. What comes out is stronger than drip coffee, but it isn't espresso in the technical sense, and it won't build a proper crema (more on that next).

You can tell the two apart from the listing alone, without opening the box. A pump machine states a bar rating, because that number is the entire selling point: the AYCHIRO and AMZCHEF both cite 20 bar, and the Chefman CraftBrew cites a 15-bar pump specifically, not just a generic "bar" figure. A steam machine has nothing to gain by quoting a number that would be unimpressive, so it doesn't — the Mr. Coffee 4-Shot is named a "Steam Espresso" maker outright, and the IMUSA 4 Cup's listing states no bar rating and no pump at all, the same absence that marks every steam machine in this guide. If a listing under $100 doesn't mention bar pressure anywhere, assume steam until proven otherwise.

One caution in the other direction: a 20-bar number on the box is a pump's maximum rating, not the pressure that actually reaches the coffee puck during a shot. Both pump machines here reach that number on paper, but so does plenty of mediocre gear at every price point — the number tells you a pump exists, not that the machine is well engineered around it.

What crema means on a machine this cheap

Crema is the reddish-brown foam that sits on top of a properly pulled shot — a mix of carbon dioxide and coffee oils emulsified by high pressure forcing its way through the grounds. It forms most reliably around 9 bar, which is exactly the pressure a steam-driven machine cannot reach. Any foam a steam machine produces on top of the cup is not crema in that technical sense; it's more likely to be from residual air or milk, not from oils pulled out of the coffee under pressure.

A pump machine rated at 15 or 20 bar can, in principle, generate real crema, because it has the mechanism crema depends on. Whether it actually does on a given shot depends on variables the bar number says nothing about — grind size, dose, tamp pressure, and how evenly the water distributes through the puck. None of the three pump machines here state a pre-infusion stage or a PID-held brew temperature, the two features that make crema more consistent on pricier machines. So expect real crema to be possible on the AYCHIRO, Chefman or AMZCHEF, but not guaranteed on every shot the way it would be on a machine built around getting that variable under control.

The practical takeaway: don't judge a sub-$100 pump machine by whether it produces café-quality crema on the first try. Judge it by whether it has the mechanism at all — and only three of the five machines here do.

Both let you steam and froth milk by hand, positioning the wand yourself and judging texture by feel rather than by an automatic sensor — a manual skill on every machine at this price, not a shortcoming specific to either one.

The Mr. Coffee 4-Shot Steam Espresso Maker takes a different approach entirely: it ships with a stainless steel frothing pitcher rather than a wand you aim yourself, which points to a separate steaming step using the machine's steam output rather than an integrated wand. None of the wands or accessories in this guide come with auto-purge, a temperature readout, or the kind of automatic microfoam control you'd find on a pricier machine. That's normal at this price and not a defect — it just means the technique is on you.

What breaks first

Sub-$100 espresso machines share a few structural weak points, and none of the five listings here state a filtration system or a built-in descaling alert, so assume you're responsible for tracking maintenance yourself on any of them.

The most common failure point across this price bracket is limescale inside a small boiler. A compact boiler heats and cools repeatedly through short brew cycles rather than holding a large reserve of hot water, and mineral buildup from tap water accelerates on exactly that kind of cycling. Our guide to water quality and descaling covers how often to run a descaling solution and why the water you use matters as much as the machine you buy.

Steam wands are the second common point of failure, because dried milk left inside the tip clogs the steam path — a problem on any machine with a wand, worse on cheaper ones without a self-purge cycle. A wand that stops frothing well is more often a cleaning issue than a hardware failure, and it's one of the dozen problems our troubleshooting guide walks through before you assume the machine needs replacing.

Plastic water reservoirs and drip trays are the third weak point on lighter, smaller-bodied machines — they crack or discolor faster than stainless components, though none of the five listings here specify housing material in enough detail to rank them on that point. Buying at this price means accepting a budget build, not accepting failure; regular descaling and a clean wand tip mostly decide which outcome you get.

When to spend $80 more instead

Adding roughly $80 to the top of this bracket — landing somewhere around $170 to $180 total — moves you into a different tier of machine, and it's worth knowing what that jump actually buys before you decide to stay under $100.

The main thing that extra money unlocks is precision you can't get here: pre-infusion stages that wet the coffee bed gently before full pressure hits it, brew-temperature control instead of a single fixed setting, or a built-in grinder so pre-ground coffee stops being a variable at all. None of the five machines in this guide offer any of those — every one of them is a fixed-temperature machine you dose and tamp by hand, with results that depend entirely on your own technique. Our guide to machines with built-in grinders covers what that next tier looks like once a grinder is part of the price.

The honest case for staying under $100 is that you don't yet know if the habit will stick. A $79.99 pump machine and a pricier one both ask you to learn the same skill — dosing, tamping, judging a shot by sight and taste — and neither does that learning for you. If you're testing the waters, the Chefman or AYCHIRO gets you a real pump to practice on for far less than finding out you don't like the routine on a $200 machine. If you already know you'll keep making espresso every morning, the extra $80 buys real headroom instead of more of the same fixed-temperature, no-pre-infusion experience you'd get here. Our guide for espresso machines for beginners covers that decision from the other direction — forgiving machines built for the learning curve rather than a bare-minimum price.

One route to skip: if espresso specifically is the goal, don't buy a $65 steam machine as a "trial" and plan to upgrade later. A steam machine can't produce what a pump machine produces no matter how well you dial it in, so the trial only tells you whether you like strong, steam-brewed coffee — a different question. If budget forces a choice, the $79.99 pump machines here answer the real question better than a cheaper steam machine does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an espresso machine under $100 actually espresso?

Only if it has a pump. Real espresso is brewed at roughly 9 bar of pressure, which requires an electric pump — the Chefman CraftBrew, AYCHIRO 20 Bar and AMZCHEF 20 Bar here all have one. The Mr. Coffee 4-Shot and IMUSA 4 Cup are steam-driven, meaning boiler steam alone pushes the water through, at pressure far below what a pump reaches. What comes out is stronger than drip coffee but isn't espresso in the technical sense.

What is the difference between a steam machine and a pump machine?

A pump machine uses an electric pump to force water through the coffee at high pressure, close to the roughly 9 bar that real espresso needs, and a listing with a stated bar rating (15-bar, 20-bar) is how you spot one. A steam machine has no pump; it relies on steam pressure building in a small boiler, which tops out well below espresso pressure and won't build genuine crema. Neither the Mr. Coffee 4-Shot nor the IMUSA 4 Cup states a bar rating, which is consistent with both being steam-driven.

Do any of these machines include a grinder?

No. None of the five machines in this guide list a built-in grinder, so budget for pre-ground espresso coffee that's correctly milled for the brew method, or a separate grinder, on top of whichever machine you buy. Our guide to machines with built-in grinders covers the next price tier up, where that feature starts to appear.

Will the steam wand on a cheap espresso machine actually froth milk well?

It will froth milk, but expect to do the work yourself — none of the wands or accessories in this guide have automatic temperature control or microfoam sensors, so texture depends on your technique regardless of which machine you buy. The AYCHIRO and Chefman both state a steam wand; the Mr. Coffee ships with a stainless steel frothing pitcher instead, a different method for the same goal. The AMZCHEF and IMUSA listings don't mention a milk accessory at all, so check the current listing before assuming one is included.
