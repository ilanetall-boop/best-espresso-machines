The short answer

Electactic currently sells two 20 bar espresso machines, and despite the shared pump rating and the shared brand name, they're built around two different bets on what you actually want from a morning espresso machine. The Electactic 20 Bar Espresso Machine with Milk Frother at $99.16 bets on milk: its listing names a steam wand, a 47 oz removable water tank and a pressure gauge, and it's marketed as a cappuccino and latte maker first. The Electactic 20 Bar Espresso Machine with Built-in Anti-Clog Coffee Grinder at $114.85 bets on the grind: the one feature its title calls out is a grinder engineered not to clog, which is the most common complaint leveled at built-in grinders generally.

They're close enough in price — about $7 apart as of our most recent checks — that the decision isn't really about budget. It's about which failure mode you'd rather avoid: inconsistent milk because you're hand-frothing with no real wand or none at all, or inconsistent grind because a cheap built-in grinder clogs and you end up back on pre-ground coffee. If lattes outnumber straight shots in your week, the milk frother model's confirmed steam wand and tank size make it the straightforward pick. If you care more about what's happening to the bean before it ever hits water, the anti-clog grinder model is solving the problem you actually have — even though its listing gives us fewer confirmed specs to compare it on.

Same price, different problem solved

It's worth sitting with how unusual this lineup is. The more common way a brand differentiates two machines is by price tier — a budget entry model and a pricier step-up with a grinder or extra milk feature bolted on, the way Philips splits its 2200 and 2300 Series by adding LatteGo milk for $310 more. Electactic instead put two machines at nearly the same price and let each one specialize: one toward milk, one toward the grind. Neither is positioned as the "better" machine or the step-up model, which means the usual shortcut — pay more, get more — doesn't apply here. You're choosing a specialization, not a tier.

That's a genuinely different shopping problem than most dual- or multi-SKU lineups present, where price usually tracks feature count fairly closely — the pattern you'll see in lineups like SHARDOR's or AYCHIRO's. The practical effect here is that neither Electactic machine makes the other one redundant the way a cheaper model often gets made redundant by a better-equipped one at $20 more. You have to actually know what matters more on a given morning — milk or grind — before the near-identical price tag tells you anything useful.

It also means this comparison can't lean on the usual "which one gives you more for your money" framing. Both machines are a 20 bar pump espresso machine at a sub-$120 price, and the honest version of the comparison is two separate feature lists pointed at two separate problems, which is the structure we'll use for the rest of this page.

The anti-clog grinder model: what it actually grinds and what it skips

The Electactic 20 Bar Espresso Machine with Built-in Anti-Clog Coffee Grinder, at $114.85, puts everything into one named feature: a grinder built to resist clogging. That's a real, specific problem with home espresso machines that integrate a grinder — fine grounds and bean oils build up in the chute and burr chamber over repeated use, especially with darker, oilier roasts, and over weeks that buildup is what jams a cheaply engineered built-in grinder. A machine marketed specifically against that failure mode is targeting someone who's already owned, or read enough complaints about, a grinder that gummed up within a few months of daily use.

What the listing doesn't give us is anything to measure that claim against. There's no grind-setting count in the product title, no stated hopper capacity, and no confirmation of burr type. We're not going to guess at any of those numbers to make this section feel more complete — if the exact grind range or hopper size is the deciding factor for you, that detail lives on the current Amazon listing page, not in our database, and it's worth checking before you commit to a model built around a grinder whose specifics you haven't yet seen.

What the title also skips is everything the milk frother model's title states plainly: no mention of a steam wand, no tank size, no pressure gauge. That doesn't mean the anti-clog grinder model lacks those things outright — titles often leave out baseline features to spotlight what's new — but we have no confirmed data saying it has them either, so we're treating them as unconfirmed rather than assuming parity with its sibling. The one thing we can say with confidence from our database is the Size/variant label: this model is listed as Medium, one step up from the milk frother model's Compact listing, which tracks with a grinder needing room for a hopper and burr assembly above the brew head.

The milk frother model: steam wand and tank size compared

The Electactic 20 Bar Espresso Machine with Milk Frother, at $99.16, is the more fully specified of the two listings, and its title reads like a checklist built for someone making cappuccinos and lattes at home: semi-automatic operation, a steam wand for frothing milk by hand, a 47 oz removable water tank, a pressure gauge, and a stainless steel body. Every one of those is stated in the product name, which is the standard we're holding both machines to throughout this page — stated, not inferred.

The steam wand is the headline difference from its sibling. A manual steam wand means you control how much air you introduce and for how long, which is also how you build genuine microfoam for a flat white rather than the looser, less adjustable foam a fixed automatic frother tends to produce. The 47 oz tank is also worth noting on its own terms: on a machine listed as Compact at this price, that's large enough to cover back-to-back drinks for two people without a mid-routine refill, assuming you're not also demanding a grinder built into the same chassis — which this model, per its listing, does not have.

Because this model doesn't name a grinder anywhere in its title, the honest read is that it ships without one, the same way a budget entry machine in most brands' lineups skips a grinder to keep the milk-and-pump hardware as the whole focus. If you buy this machine, plan on pre-ground espresso roast or a separate burr grinder — a category cost that typically runs $80 to $200 on its own, not a number specific to this listing.

Pressure gauge and build, side by side

Both machines share the 20 bar pump rating, and it's worth repeating the same caveat we give on every machine in this category: 20 bar is the pump's maximum rating, not the pressure delivered to the coffee. Espresso brews at roughly 9 bar at the puck regardless of what the pump could theoretically reach, so a shared "20 bar" spec tells you the two machines are using similarly rated pumps, not that either one brews better espresso than a machine rated at 9 bar would. Neither listing names its pump manufacturer either, which is a separate detail from the bar rating — see what naming a pump brand actually tells you if you want to go deeper on what that detail is worth when a listing does include it.

Where the two machines actually diverge, on the data we have, is in what's visible and confirmed about the rest of the build. The milk frother model's title states a pressure gauge and a stainless steel body outright — a pressure gauge on a home machine typically shows you where the pump is operating during a shot, a genuinely useful diagnostic for noticing when a puck is packed too tight or too loose, even if it's not a guarantee of a better shot on its own. The anti-clog grinder model's title states neither. That's not evidence the Medium-sized grinder model skips a gauge or uses a different chassis material; it's evidence that we don't have confirmed data saying it has either, and we'd rather tell you that plainly than paper over the gap with an assumption.

If a visible pressure gauge matters to you as a feedback tool while you're learning to pull shots, the milk frother model is the one we can confirm has it. If it doesn't matter to you — some people never watch the gauge after the first week — it's a feature you'd be paying for either way on that model, so it's worth weighing against what you'd be giving up by skipping the anti-clog grinder model's one named feature.

Which Electactic model matches beans-first versus milk-first mornings

Most of this decision comes down to a habit you already have, not a spec you need to go learn. If your actual morning routine is a shot or two of straight espresso, maybe with a splash of milk stirred in rather than steamed, the grinder is the part of the machine doing the most work on taste — and the anti-clog grinder model is the one built to keep that part running without jamming on you six months in. You're accepting more unknowns in exchange for solving the specific failure you're most likely to hit.

If your actual morning routine includes a latte or a cappuccino most days, the steam wand is the part of the machine you'll touch every single time, and it's also the one piece of hardware where "built-in but unconfirmed" isn't good enough — you want to know it's there before you buy. The milk frother model's listing confirms it; the anti-clog grinder model's doesn't. In that case the $99.16 model is the lower-risk buy regardless of the roughly $7 price gap, because you're not gambling on an unlisted feature for the part of the routine you use daily.

There's a third case worth naming honestly: if you want both a reliable built-in grinder and a confirmed steam wand in one machine, neither Electactic model gives you that combination — the two listings simply don't overlap that way. Machines that carry both at once tend to sit a bit higher up, in the band our guide to machines between $250 and $350 covers, and if that combination is non-negotiable for you, it's worth comparing one of those against spending roughly the same $108–$115 on an Electactic plus a separate grinder or a separate milk frother, rather than assuming either Electactic model will quietly cover the gap.

The Size/variant difference — Compact for the milk frother model, Medium for the grinder model — is a small additional data point worth factoring in if counter space is tight. We don't have exact dimensions for either, so measure against the live listing rather than those two words alone, but a Medium listing on a grinder-equipped machine is the expected shape: room has to go somewhere for the hopper and burr mechanism.
