The short answer

All three machines in this lineup start from the same place: a 20-bar pump pushing water through a stainless-steel body. What SHARDOR changes between the $79.99, $119.99 and $189.99 tiers is the instrumentation on the front and, at the top, whether the machine grinds your beans for you. That's a cleaner ladder than most brands offer, because each step adds something specific rather than a vague "premium" label.

The $79.99 model (ASIN B0GTYHJBKW) is the entry point: a visible pressure gauge, NTC-based temperature sensing, an LED touchscreen, and a 45 oz tank. The $119.99 model (ASIN B0F99GMMRL) drops the gauge and touchscreen in favor of an LCD display, grows the tank to 60 oz, adds a cup warmer, and heats in a claimed 30 seconds. The $189.99 model (ASIN B0HJ1N8KXD) is the only one that changes the actual workflow: it adds a built-in grinder with 45 grind settings, PID temperature control holding a stated 194–204°F band, a cold-and-hot brew mode, and a 60.9 oz tank.

The shared foundation

Before getting into what's different, it's worth being clear about what's identical across the line, because it's easy to assume a $110 price spread means three unrelated machines. It doesn't.

Every model here runs a 20-bar pump in a stainless-steel body, and every model draws roughly 1350W. None of that changes as you move up the lineup. The 20-bar figure is worth a brief note before it does any work in your decision: espresso brews at roughly 9 bar of pressure at the coffee puck, so a 20-bar rating describes the pump's maximum output, not the pressure that reaches your cup. All three machines have more pump than any of them will use during a normal shot — which means the number tells you nothing about which of the three pulls a better espresso. Our guide to pressure gauges under $130 goes deeper on why a higher bar rating isn't a meaningful upgrade on its own, across brands.

Where the line diverges even in the basics is how each model handles frothing milk. SHARDOR's own listing calls the $79.99 model's attachment a milk frother; the $119.99 and $189.99 models are both listed with a steam wand. We don't have a teardown that confirms whether these are mechanically the same part under a different label, so we're reporting the terms as listed rather than assuming they're identical. If a manual steam wand specifically matters to you — more control, a slight learning curve — confirm the attachment type on the current listing before buying the gauge model.

The $79.99 model: pressure gauge, NTC temperature control, touchscreen

At $79.99, this is SHARDOR's entry point and the cheapest of the three by a wide margin — $40 under the LCD model and $110 under the grinder model. For that price it includes a visible pressure gauge on the front panel, NTC-based temperature sensing, an LED touchscreen for controls, and a 45 oz water tank.

The gauge is the headline feature, and it's worth being precise about what it does and doesn't tell you. It's a mechanical dial plumbed into the water line, and while the pump runs it shows line pressure — a rough read on how hard the pump is pushing against the resistance of the basket and the compressed coffee inside it. That's a genuinely useful sanity check on a machine with no other feedback, but it can't tell you whether the shot is actually dialed in, because two different pucks can produce two different needle positions on the identical machine, and neither position says which shot tasted better. Treat it as a secondary signal after taste and shot time, not a diagnostic tool.

NTC stands for negative temperature coefficient — a type of thermistor that measures water temperature and lets the machine's control board react to changes. It's the standard, lower-cost way of managing brew temperature on budget machines, and it works by sensing and correcting rather than by holding a tight target band the way the PID system on the $189.99 model does. We'll come back to that distinction in the grinder section, because it's the more consequential one.

The touchscreen is mostly about how you interact with the machine rather than what it brews. An LED touchscreen reads cleaner than a row of physical buttons and tends to be easier to wipe down, but it doesn't change pump pressure, temperature stability, or tank capacity. At 45 oz, the tank here is also the smallest of the three — noticeably so next to the two larger models' 60 oz-plus capacity, which matters if more than one person in the house is drinking espresso.

The $119.99 model: LCD display, 60oz tank, cup warmer

Forty dollars above the gauge model sits the LCD version, and the swap here is mostly about information and capacity rather than brewing mechanics. The pressure gauge is gone, replaced by an LCD display; the touchscreen controls give way to whatever interface SHARDOR builds around that display. In exchange, the tank grows from 45 oz to 60 oz — a meaningful jump if your household goes through more than a couple of shots a day — and the machine adds a cup warmer and a claimed 30-second fast heat-up. It also explicitly supports single and double shots, and lists latte and cappuccino among its drink options alongside the steam wand.

None of that touches what actually happens to the coffee during extraction. A cup warmer keeps a porcelain cup at a stable temperature before you pour, which matters for how long your espresso stays hot in the cup but has no effect on the shot itself — it's a comfort feature, not a brewing one. A 30-second heat-up, if it holds up in practice, mainly affects how long you wait before your first shot of the day; it doesn't change the pump, the pressure, or the temperature stability during the pull.

The bigger tank is the one spec here that's a genuine, if modest, functional upgrade: 60 oz versus 45 oz means fewer trips back to the sink if the machine is in regular use by more than one person. Everything else in this tier is best understood as a nicer way to operate the same underlying 20-bar pump rather than a change to what that pump produces. The $189.99 jump: a built-in grinder with PID control

The next $70 buys a materially different machine, not just a nicer version of the same one. The $189.99 model adds a built-in grinder with 45 settings, swaps NTC sensing for PID temperature control holding a stated 194–204°F band, adds a cold-and-hot brew mode, and grows the tank again to 60.9 oz. It keeps the steam wand from the LCD tier.

Start with the grinder, because it's the feature that actually changes the sequence of making a cup. On the two cheaper models you're supplying your own ground coffee — pre-ground from a bag, or from a separate grinder you run beforehand. On this model, beans go in and the machine grinds them fresh, immediately before brewing, with 45 settings to tune particle size. Espresso is unusually sensitive to grind size: slightly too coarse and the shot runs fast and thin; slightly too fine and it chokes and turns bitter. A dedicated grinder with that many adjustment points gives you far more control over that variable than buying pre-ground coffee ever could, regardless of which machine you pair it with.

PID control is the second real upgrade, and it's worth explaining in plain terms because it's easy to read past as marketing language. PID stands for proportional-integral-derivative — a control algorithm that continuously measures the gap between the current temperature and the target, and adjusts the heating element in small, ongoing corrections to hold that target steady, rather than just reacting after the fact the way simpler NTC sensing does. In practice, that generally means less temperature swing between shots and less drift over a brewing session — both of which affect extraction consistency, since brew temperature is one of the handful of variables that determines how a shot tastes. The stated 194–204°F range on this model sits within the band most specialty coffee guidance treats as suitable for espresso extraction.

The cold-and-hot brew mode is the one spec here we can describe but not fully unpack: SHARDOR's listing states the machine supports both, without detailing how the cold mode is achieved mechanically. We'll report that it's offered and leave the "how" to the listing, rather than guess at a brewing method we can't verify. The 60.9 oz tank is effectively the same practical capacity as the LCD model's 60 oz — not a meaningful difference on its own.

Which upgrade actually changes your morning versus which just looks nicer

Sorting these upgrades by whether they touch the coffee or just the experience of using the machine makes the ladder much easier to reason about.

Purely cosmetic or convenience, with no effect on the shot: the switch from a pressure gauge to an LCD display is a change in what information you see, not in what the pump does. The touchscreen-versus-LCD question is the same kind of swap — both are interfaces for the same 20-bar pump underneath. The cup warmer on the $119.99 model keeps a cup hot before you pour; it does nothing to the espresso while it's brewing. A faster claimed heat-up time affects how long you wait, not what comes out once the machine is ready.

Real, but modest: the tank size increase from 45 oz to 60-plus oz means fewer refills, which matters if the machine sees heavy daily use, but it has no bearing on shot quality for any single cup.

The one upgrade that changes the cup: the built-in grinder on the $189.99 model. Grind freshness and grind size are two of the most consequential variables in espresso extraction, and buying pre-ground coffee for either of the cheaper two models puts a hard ceiling on what they can produce no matter how precisely their other specs are tuned. PID temperature control is the second cup-relevant change — holding a stable 194–204°F band shot after shot is a meaningfully different proposition from NTC sensing reacting to temperature after it's already drifted.

Put plainly: if your budget stops at $119.99, you're choosing between two machines that brew essentially the same way and differ mainly in how they look and how much water they hold. If you can stretch to $189.99, you're choosing a machine that changes what's possible before the water even starts moving.

Frequently asked questions

Is the $189.99 SHARDOR with a grinder worth $110 more than the $79.99 gauge model?

If you're currently using pre-ground coffee or would need to buy a separate burr grinder anyway, yes — the built-in grinder and the PID temperature control are the two upgrades on this page that actually change what lands in the cup, while the pressure gauge, touchscreen and LCD display differences between the two cheaper models don't. If you already own a good grinder, the case weakens, since you'd be paying again for a feature you have elsewhere.

What does PID temperature control add over NTC control?

NTC sensing measures water temperature with a thermistor and reacts after the temperature has already drifted. PID control continuously compares the current temperature against a target and makes small, ongoing corrections to hold that target steady — in general, that means less swing between shots and less drift over a session, both of which affect extraction consistency. The $189.99 SHARDOR model states a 194–204°F PID-controlled range; the $79.99 model uses NTC sensing instead.

Do I need the 60 oz tank, or is 45 oz enough?

It depends on how many people in the household drink espresso and how often. A 45 oz tank is fine for one person pulling one or two shots a day without constant refills; once you're making drinks for two or more people regularly, the 60 oz-plus tanks on the LCD and grinder models mean noticeably fewer trips back to the sink.

Is a touchscreen better than an LCD display on an espresso machine?

Neither changes what the pump or the heating element does — both are just the interface you use to control the same underlying 20-bar machine. A touchscreen tends to be easier to wipe clean; an LCD display is a more traditional readout. Pick based on which you prefer to use day to day, not on any expectation that one brews a better shot than the other.
