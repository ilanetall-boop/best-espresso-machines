The short answer

If you just want the shape of the decision: both EUHOMY machines in this comparison give you a built-in grinder and a milk frother in one appliance, and both are listed in the brand's "Large" size variant, so footprint isn't what separates them. The base model (B0FVWWW95P) states its pump pressure outright — 20 bar — and costs $309.99. The Automatic model (B0H7HMMLJX) drops the bar-pressure figure from its own title and adds the word "Automatic" instead, at $368.56.

That's a real difference in how EUHOMY chose to describe the two machines, and it's the only differentiator either listing gives us beyond price. We don't have a spec sheet that spells out grind-setting counts or frother mechanism for either machine, so rather than guess at what's inside the chassis, we'll walk through what the naming itself tells you, what's identical between the two as far as the listings go, and which one actually fits a modest daily routine.

The shared formula: grinder plus frother

Both machines commit to the same core idea before you get anywhere near price: fold a burr grinder and a milk-frothing function into one chassis, rather than selling a bare pump machine and leaving those two jobs to separate purchases. That's the formula behind most bean-to-cup machines in this price bracket, and EUHOMY has applied it to its entire current espresso lineup rather than splitting a cheap bare machine from a pricier grinder model the way some brands do.

A grinder matters here for the same reason it matters on any espresso machine: pre-ground coffee is milled to a single compromise size meant to work across every brew method, which is a poor fit for espresso specifically and stales within days regardless of how it's packaged. A milk frother matters for the same reason it matters on any machine aimed at lattes and cappuccinos rather than straight shots — without one, milk drinks fall back on a separate pitcher and a stovetop or handheld whisk. By putting both in the box on every model it sells, EUHOMY has made "do I need to buy anything else" close to a non-question across its range, at either price.

What neither listing specifies is the exact number of grind settings, the frothing mechanism (a manual wand you steam with by hand, or an automated frothing cycle), the tank size, or the wattage. We're not going to assign either machine a number we don't have — if that level of detail matters to your decision, it's a question for the live Amazon listing before you buy, not something this page can answer for you.

What "Automatic" actually changes on the name

The single word that separates these two titles is "Automatic," and it's worth taking seriously rather than skipping past, because it's the only differentiator EUHOMY itself put in writing. The base model is named the "EUHOMY Espresso Machine with Grinder and Milk Frother, 20 Bar Pressure." The pricier one is named the "EUHOMY Automatic Espresso Machine with Built-in Grinder and Milk Frother" — same grinder-plus-frother formula, but "Automatic" replaces the bar-pressure number as the headline claim.

In the espresso category broadly, "automatic" usually describes how much of the brewing cycle the machine runs on its own once you start it — stopping a shot at a programmed volume instead of requiring you to watch and cut it off by eye, for instance, or running a frothing cycle on a timer rather than needing a hand on the wand the whole time. That's general background about what the word tends to signal across the category, not a confirmed feature list for this specific machine — EUHOMY's listing doesn't spell out which part of the process the automation covers, and we're not going to fill that gap with a guess.

What we can say with more confidence is what the naming choice implies about marketing emphasis. The base model's title sells on a number — 20 bar, which sounds more technical than it is, since espresso is actually brewed at roughly 9 bar at the coffee itself and a 20-bar rating describes the pump's maximum rather than brewing pressure. The Automatic model's title sells on a word instead, aimed at buyers who want less to think about during the brew rather than a bigger number on the spec sheet. Whether that's worth $59.63 more is a question about your own patience at the machine, not about which spec sheet is longer. It's the same lesson we draw in our look at a listing that puts its country of manufacture in the title: what a brand chooses to foreground in a product name tells you what it wants to sell you on, not the full spec sheet underneath it.

Which one fits a daily one-or-two-cup routine

For a single person pulling one or two shots a day — the most common home routine, and the one where a machine's ceiling rarely gets tested — the gap between these two machines is smaller in practice than $59.63 suggests. A low daily volume doesn't stress a grinder's hopper, doesn't demand back-to-back milk drinks, and doesn't push a pump hard enough for small, unstated differences in automation to show up as saved time worth noticing. At that pace, the base model's stated 20-bar pump and built-in grinder cover the job the same way the Automatic's do, for $59.63 less.

The case for paying the Automatic's premium in a one-or-two-cup routine isn't volume — it's friction at a specific moment in the day. If the appeal of "automatic" is genuinely about doing less yourself each morning rather than about handling more drinks, that's a legitimate reason to pay more even at low volume, because the benefit is per-cup, not per-day-total. It's also the harder case to verify from a listing alone: you're paying for a word, not a number, and the only way to know whether that word delivers what you expect is to read the live listing's own description and spec sheet closely before you order.

Where the math clearly favors the base model is a household with more than one or two cups a day, simply because at $309.99 against $368.56 you're starting $59.63 ahead, and neither listing gives you a stated reason tied to higher volume to prefer the pricier one.
