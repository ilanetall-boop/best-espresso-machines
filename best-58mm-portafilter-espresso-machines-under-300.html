Why 58mm matters

Portafilter diameter is one of the least glamorous specs on an espresso machine and one of the most consequential once you own it. The number refers to the basket and portafilter that lock into the group head — the part loaded with ground coffee before a shot — and 58mm is the size nearly every commercial café machine uses, a holdover from the La Marzocco and E61-style designs that still define professional equipment. Decades of cafés standardizing on that one size is why the aftermarket around it runs so much deeper than anything built around a proprietary diameter.

Concretely, that means three things are easy to buy for a 58mm machine and genuinely hard to find for most other sizes: precision baskets from third-party makers that distribute water more evenly than many stock baskets, bottomless — or "naked" — portafilters that let you watch the extraction stream and catch channeling before it ruins the cup, and tampers that fit without the half-millimeter wobble that comes from hunting down an off-brand size. Breville's 54mm and the 51mm baskets common on many budget semi-automatics work perfectly well out of the box, but step outside what shipped in the box and the accessory shelf narrows fast — fewer third-party basket options, fewer bottomless portafilters sized to fit, sometimes none at all depending on the exact variant.

That's the real argument for paying attention to this number rather than treating it as a spec-sheet footnote. A $300 ceiling on a 58mm machine buys the same starting point a commercial kitchen works from, and a clear upgrade path if you ever want a different basket than the one in the box. HIBREW and Gevi both state 58mm directly in their listing names; AMZCHEF and Tuni are grouped into this same bracket on the same basis, though their listing titles lead with the grinder and boiler features rather than the basket number — worth a quick check against the current Amazon listing if portafilter diameter is the one spec you're buying for.

What usually gets cut to hit 58mm under $300

58mm group heads are physically larger than 51mm or 54mm ones — more metal, a wider boiler interface, a heavier portafilter — and all of that costs more to manufacture than a smaller equivalent. Machines in this bracket pay for the larger format by trimming something else, and the pattern across these four listings is consistent: it's the spec sheet itself that shrinks first.

Wattage is stated for exactly one of the four machines here, AMZCHEF's 1500W. Tank size is stated for none of them. Footprint is stated for exactly one, Gevi's 16.61 x 15.66 x 18.85 inches. HIBREW's customizable pressure release

The HIBREW H10 Customizable 58mm Espresso Machine, One-Touch Pressure Release is the only machine in this guide built around a single named mechanism rather than a spread of features, and the name tells you exactly what that mechanism is. A pressure release — often called an OPV, or over-pressure valve, on machines that have one — vents brew pressure out of the group head, typically once a shot finishes, so the puck isn't still sitting under pressure when you unlock the portafilter to knock it out. Without one, pulling the portafilter immediately after a shot can spit hot coffee and grounds back at you; with one, a quick purge drops the pressure first.

What HIBREW adds, per its own listing name, is that the release is customizable and one-touch rather than fixed — you control when the purge happens rather than the machine running it on a fixed timer, and it's a single action rather than a multi-step process. At $170.99, that's a genuinely specific feature to build a listing name around, and it sits at the lower end of this four-machine range: only AMZCHEF, at $199.48, costs less.

What the listing doesn't state is a grinder. If the HIBREW is on your shortlist, budget for pairing it with a separate burr grinder — the same bring-your-own-grinder route covered in our guide to visible bean hoppers, which looks at the built-in side of that same decision.

Gevi and Tuni: the two dual boilers compared

A dual boiler is the rarest feature in this entire price bracket, and this guide has two of them: the Gevi Dual Boiler Espresso Machine With Grinder & 58mm Portafilter at $270.44, and the Tuni E2 Gen3 Dual Boiler Espresso Machine with Super+ Steam Power at $241.44. Both solve the same underlying problem — a single boiler has to switch between brewing temperature and steaming temperature, so pulling a shot right before or after frothing milk usually means a wait while the machine catches up. Two independent boilers remove that wait: one stays at brew temperature, the other at steam temperature, continuously.

Where they diverge is everything built around that shared feature. Gevi pairs its dual boiler with a built-in grinder, so the $270.44 price covers grinding, dual-boiler brewing and the 58mm portafilter in one unit — the only machine in this guide that bundles all three. Its listing also states an actual footprint, 16.61 inches deep by 15.66 wide by 18.85 tall, the only one of the four to publish exact dimensions; a dual boiler plus a grinder hopper is a lot to fit on one chassis, and that's the number to check against your counter and any cabinet above it before ordering.

Tuni's E2 Gen3 skips the grinder — its listing doesn't name one — and spends its $241.44 on the dual boiler plus what the brand calls Super+ Steam Power, language that points specifically at the steaming side of the two boilers rather than the brew side. If your priority is milk — lattes, flat whites, back-to-back drinks for more than one person — a dual boiler with steam power called out by name is a more direct match than one bundled with a grinder you may already own. If your priority is one box that handles the grinding too, Gevi's $270.44 does more for less. AMZCHEF's 40 grind settings against the other three

Grind-settings count is one of the most useful numbers for judging how finely a machine lets you dial in, because the gap between a sour, under-extracted shot and a bitter, over-extracted one is often a click or two wide on a home grinder — more settings mean more chances to land in that window rather than straddle it. Of the four machines in this guide, exactly one states a number: AMZCHEF's 40.

The other three don't publish a settings count at all, for different reasons. HIBREW and Tuni don't name a grinder in their listings, so there's nothing to count — if you pair either with a separate burr grinder, the settings question moves to whichever standalone grinder you choose instead. Gevi does include a built-in grinder, which makes its silence on settings count the more notable gap: it's the one direct comparison point where AMZCHEF's listing simply says more. That doesn't mean Gevi's grinder is worse — grind range and step size vary independently of how many settings a dial has, and a coarser total range split into 40 steps can still be less useful than a wider range split into fewer — it means AMZCHEF is the only one of the four willing to put a number on the page.

For a buyer choosing between AMZCHEF and Gevi specifically, that asymmetry is worth weighing against the rest of each spec sheet: AMZCHEF is roughly $60 cheaper and names its grind-settings count; Gevi costs more but adds a second boiler and a stated footprint. Whether 40 named settings beats an unstated count paired with a dual boiler depends on which of those two features you'd actually use on a weekday morning.

When a 51mm or 54mm basket is still the smarter buy

None of this is an argument that 58mm is always the right basket size to buy. It's the size with the deepest aftermarket, which matters a great deal if you plan to swap baskets, add a bottomless portafilter, or upgrade accessories over the years — and matters much less if you're buying one machine, running the basket it ships with, and never touching the aftermarket at all.

54mm, mostly Breville's proprietary size, and 51mm, common across a wide swath of budget semi-automatics, both have real advantages that have nothing to do with aftermarket depth. Smaller baskets generally mean a smaller group head, which generally means a smaller, lighter overall machine — relevant if counter space is the actual constraint rather than basket diameter. They're also frequently cheaper to manufacture, which is a real part of why machines well under $200 can exist at all; our guide to grinder-equipped machines under $150 covers three of them, none at 58mm, all considerably cheaper than anything in this guide.

The smaller size is also the smarter buy if you're not yet confident espresso will stick as a habit. Spending less now and finding out whether you'll actually use a machine every morning is a better bet than paying a premium for an aftermarket ecosystem you may never touch. The 58mm upgrade path is still there later — a first machine isn't a permanent decision, and the standalone grinder or bottomless portafilter you'd buy for a 58mm machine works just as well on your second one as it did on your first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a 58mm portafilter actually get me that a 51mm or 54mm one doesn't?

Mainly a deeper aftermarket. 58mm is the diameter most commercial café machines use, so third-party precision baskets, bottomless portafilters and tampers are made for it far more often than for proprietary sizes like Breville's 54mm or the 51mm common on budget semi-automatics. The machine itself doesn't necessarily brew better espresso at 58mm — it gives you more room to upgrade later without hunting for a size that barely exists in the aftermarket.

Is HIBREW's one-touch pressure release the same thing as PID or pre-infusion?

No. A pressure release, or OPV, vents brew pressure out of the group head, typically after a shot finishes, so the puck isn't still under pressure when you unlock the portafilter. PID is temperature control and pre-infusion is a gentle start to the brew cycle — different mechanisms entirely. HIBREW's listing names the pressure release specifically and doesn't claim PID or pre-infusion, so we haven't attributed either to it.

What's the real difference between Gevi's dual boiler and Tuni's?

Both give the brew and steam sides independent temperatures instead of one shared boiler, which is the core benefit of the feature either way. Gevi pairs it with a built-in grinder and a stated footprint, for $270.44. Tuni skips the grinder, names its steam output specifically as Super+ Steam Power, and costs $241.44 — the higher price buys more steam-focused branding and two boilers, not a grinder.

Should I buy the cheapest 58mm machine here, or spend more?

That depends on which feature you'd actually use. AMZCHEF, at $199.48, is the cheapest and the only one with a stated grind-settings count. If a dual boiler or a confirmed footprint matters more to your routine than saving roughly $60, Gevi or Tuni are built around those features specifically rather than around price.
