How to Clean and Descale an Espresso Machine (So It Lasts)

Almost every home machine that dies young dies of one of three things: rancid coffee oil, cooked milk, or limescale. None of them need more than a few minutes a week to prevent.

By Ilane Tall · Updated August 12, 2026 · Research-based.

Some links on this page are affiliate links: if you buy through them we may earn a commission, at no extra cost to you. We don't recommend cleaning products by name because we have no verified data on them — use whatever your machine's manufacturer specifies.

An espresso machine does something no other kitchen appliance does: push near-boiling water at roughly 9 bar through a bed of oily grounds, then blast steam through milk, several times a day, for years. Every part of that job leaves something behind. Coffee is oily, and those oils coat the basket, the shower screen and the group. Milk is protein and sugar, and both bake onto hot metal. Tap water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium, and heat drops them out of solution as hard, insulating crust.

Neglect doesn't announce itself. There's no warning light for a group screen glazed with oxidized oil. The machine just quietly gets worse: shots go bitter and hollow, heat-up creeps from 30 seconds to two minutes, the pump starts to whine, and one morning nothing comes out. By then the repair estimate often approaches the price of a replacement — which is why maintenance is the highest-return habit in home espresso, ahead of better beans and well ahead of a better machine.

What follows is the whole ladder as a routine you can keep: two minutes a day, fifteen minutes a week, half an hour a month, and a descale on the schedule your water sets. One honest note first, because it shapes everything here: we compare specifications, box contents and prices, we don't run a testing lab, and we don't publish ratings we can't verify. Nothing below is a summary of review sentiment — it's mechanism, plus the published features of the machines in our main espresso machine guide. Where your manual disagrees with us, your manual wins. It's the only document written for your exact boiler, seals and pump.

Every shot, every day

The two-minute routine that does most of the work

This is the part that matters. If you only ever do the daily routine and a descale when the manual tells you to, your machine will outlive most of its peers.

Purge the group before and after

Run a two- or three-second flush of water through the empty group before you lock in the portafilter, and again after you knock out the puck. The first flush stabilizes the temperature of the group and rinses off grounds sitting on the shower screen; the second washes away the fine layer of coffee mud the shot just deposited. On a thermoblock machine this also brings fresh water into the brew path instead of leaving stale water sitting in it overnight.

Knock out the puck, then rinse and dry the basket

Dump the puck immediately, while it's still a solid disc — it comes out cleanly and the basket doesn't have time to varnish. Rinse the basket under the tap and wipe it dry. The reason is the perforations: a basket's holes are tiny, and coffee fines plus oil dry into them like grout. Half-blocked holes force water through the remaining ones, which is a slow drift toward channeled, uneven, sour shots. If you're chasing consistency, dirty baskets are one of the quiet variables behind it, which is worth knowing before you start blaming your grind in the shot-dialing process.

Why old coffee oil is a taste problem, not just a hygiene one

Coffee's oils oxidize on contact with air and accelerate when they're kept warm — exactly the conditions inside a group head. Oxidized, rancid oil tastes bitter and stale, and because it sits in the path every drop of your espresso travels through, it re-contaminates each shot. This is the mechanism behind the machine that "used to make better coffee." No fresh, expensive bean can survive being filtered through a year of rancid residue. You can't taste your way out of it; you can only clean it out.

Wipe the steam wand immediately, then purge it

The instant you finish steaming, wipe the wand with a damp cloth kept only for that job, then open the steam valve for a second to blow out the milk that gets drawn back into the tip as it cools. Do it in that order and do it now, not after you drink your coffee.

Milk's proteins denature and its sugars caramelize on hot brass. Within a minute or two, milk on a hot wand goes from wipeable to a hard, yellowed lacquer that water won't touch — at that point it needs a soak in a dedicated milk-line cleaner, and the tip holes clog. A partially blocked tip is the most common cause of a wand that "stopped steaming properly": the steam is there, the holes aren't. Machines with automatic frothing, like the Bambino's temperature-controlled wand, still need the same wipe-and-purge; automation handles the texturing, not the cleaning.

Empty the drip tray and leave the portafilter out

Empty and rinse the drip tray daily. It collects sugary milk drips and coffee water, and in a warm kitchen that mixture starts smelling within a couple of days. Then leave the portafilter sitting out of the group to dry, rather than clamped in it. Two reasons: a locked-in portafilter keeps the group gasket compressed and hot around the clock, which hardens it faster, and a shut group traps moisture against whatever coffee residue is still in there. Both are free to avoid.

Once a week

The weekly deep clean, and the truth about backflushing

Set aside fifteen minutes. Most of it is soaking time, so you can start it and walk away.

Soak the basket and portafilter

Pop the basket out of the portafilter and put both into a bowl of hot water with a coffee-oil detergent — the alkaline powders or tablets sold for espresso equipment, not dish soap, which isn't formulated to break down oxidized coffee oil and tends to leave a soapy taste behind. Fifteen to twenty minutes is usually enough; you'll see the water go brown. Then brush both, paying attention to the basket's rim and the underside of the portafilter's spouts, and rinse very thoroughly. Two points of caution: check your manual before submerging a portafilter with a plastic handle in hot water, and don't put espresso parts in the dishwasher unless the manual explicitly allows it — dishwasher detergent is aggressive to aluminum and to some coatings.

Brush the group gasket and shower screen

With the machine warm but not brewing, run a stiff nylon brush around the group gasket — the rubber ring the portafilter seals against — and across the shower screen. You'll dislodge a surprising quantity of stale grounds packed into the gasket's lip. If your shower screen is held by a central screw and your manual says it's removable, take it off weekly or monthly, soak it in the same detergent solution and hold it up to a light to check every hole is clear. If it isn't removable, brush it and flush water through it instead.

Backflushing: which machines can, and which can't

Backflushing is the most effective way to clean a group, and also the most misunderstood. It requires a three-way solenoid valve — the part that releases the pressure trapped above the puck at the end of a shot and dumps it to the drip tray, which is why machines that have one leave you a dry, knockable puck. If your machine has that valve, you fit a blind disc (a basket with no holes, sometimes called a blank or cleaning disc) into the portafilter, lock it in, and run the pump. Water has nowhere to go, so it backs up through the shower screen and out through the three-way valve, carrying dissolved oils out of the parts you can't reach. Add the manufacturer's cleaning tablet or detergent for a chemical backflush, then repeat several times with clean water until nothing coloured comes out.

Here's the part most guides skip: many entry-level machines cannot be backflushed at all. Machines built around a pressurized (dual-wall) basket and without a three-way valve have no drain path, so blocking the outlet just makes the pump strain against a dead end — at best it does nothing useful, at worst you're stressing the pump and pushing water past seals. The reliable test isn't the price of the machine, it's the documentation: look in your manual's index for "backflush", or for a supplied blind or cleaning disc in the box contents. If neither exists, treat your machine as manual-clean only, and get your group cleaning from soaking the basket, brushing the screen and flushing plain water. Among the machines in our guide, the Breville Barista Express is documented with a cleaning disc, cleaning tablets and a brush tool in the box; the sub-$150 machines like the Chefman CraftBrew and the CASABREWS models list no such accessories, so assume manual cleaning unless their manuals say otherwise. If you want the full logic of what separates these tiers, we lay it out in the machine-classes guide.

Wash the water tank

Empty the tank, wash it with warm water and a little mild soap, rinse it well and let it air-dry. Standing water in a warm plastic reservoir grows a biofilm — that faint slimy feel on the inside walls — and it's the start of every "my coffee tastes musty" mystery. Removable tanks make this a thirty-second job, which is a maintenance feature people undervalue when shopping: the AMZCHEF's 60 oz tank and the Chefman CraftBrew's 1.5 L removable reservoir both lift out. Also, don't leave water sitting in the tank for weeks. If the machine won't be used, empty it.

Wipe the bean hopper and grinder chute — dry

Bean oils build a sticky film inside a hopper, and that film goes stale and taints fresh beans. Empty it weekly and wipe it with a dry cloth or a paper towel. The absolute rule for the grinding side: never put water inside a grinder. Water plus coffee dust makes a paste that sets hard in the chute and around the burr carrier, and moisture on steel burrs invites rust. Use a dry brush and, if you have one, a vacuum. That applies equally to a built-in grinder — Breville's half-pound hopper, the AMZCHEF's 44-setting grinder, the Gevi's 30-setting conical burr — and to a separate one, a trade-off we go through in built-in versus separate grinders.

Monthly and quarterly

Burrs, gaskets and filters: the parts that fail slowly

Clean the grinder burrs

Ground coffee leaves fines and oil in the burr chamber, and old grounds retained there get pushed into your next dose. Monthly is a reasonable default for a household pulling a shot or two a day; if you drink dark, oily roasts, sooner. Empty the hopper, run the grinder briefly with nothing in it to clear the chamber, then follow the manual to remove the upper burr — on most consumer machines it's a quarter-turn or a couple of screws — and brush out the burr carrier and the chute. Purpose-made grinder cleaning pellets are the low-effort option where the manual allows them. Skip the old internet trick of grinding rice: it's harder than coffee and it's a good way to damage burrs or a motor. Reassemble exactly as the manual shows, because burr alignment is what your grind settings are calibrated against.

Inspect the group gasket

The group gasket is a rubber or silicone ring living permanently at brew temperature, and heat cycles harden it. As it hardens it stops deforming to seal, and the classic symptom appears: water sprays or dribbles from the edge of the portafilter mid-shot, sometimes with a hiss. A second tell is the handle position drifting — you find yourself pushing the portafilter further and further around to get it tight. A gasket is one of the cheapest parts in the machine and, on most models, a user-replaceable one. Under daily use, plan on inspecting it every few months and replacing it roughly annually rather than waiting for it to fail. A hardened gasket also masks itself as a bad-shot problem, since a leaking seal drops brew pressure at the puck.

Replace the water filter, if your machine has one

Some machines ship with an in-tank water filter — the Breville BES870XL, for example, includes a water filter with a holder. These use carbon and ion-exchange media to cut chlorine taste and reduce some of the minerals that form scale, and they exhaust on a schedule set by volume, typically expressed in the manual as a number of tank-fills or months. An exhausted filter gives you no warning at all: it simply stops reducing anything while looking identical. Put the replacement date in the same calendar as your descale. And note the interaction covered in the next section: most manuals tell you to remove the water filter before running a descaling cycle, because descaling solution passing through the media will strip it.

If your machine is a super-automatic or a capsule machine

The monthly list changes shape. On a super-automatic like the De'Longhi Magnifica Start, most cleaning is automated cycles plus emptying the used-puck container and the internal drip tray — the machine drops a compressed puck into an internal bin after every drink, and if you don't empty it on schedule it jams the brew unit. Your job is largely to obey the machine's prompts, remove and rinse the brew unit and the milk frother parts per the manual, and never ignore a rinse or descale request. On a capsule machine like the Ninja CFN601 there's no group head to clean at all: maintenance is descaling on schedule, cleaning the frother after every milk drink, emptying the used-capsule bin, and wiping the capsule holder and the piercing area where grounds accumulate.

The big one

Descaling, properly — and why generic recipes are a bad idea

What limescale actually is

Tap water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium compounds. When you heat it, those compounds come out of solution and plate themselves onto the hottest surfaces in the machine: the heating element, the boiler walls, and the narrow internal passages of a thermoblock or thermocoil. That deposit is limescale — chalky, hard, and a poor conductor of heat.

Two consequences follow, and both are gradual enough to feel like the machine "getting old." First, an insulating layer between the element and the water means longer heat-up times and lower, less stable brew temperature. Second, and worse, the scale narrows the water path. Thermoblock machines — which is most machines under $700 — heat water in thin channels, so they have very little room to lose. Flow drops, the pump has to work against a restriction and starts to whine, and eventually a passage blocks completely. That's the failure that turns into a repair quote that can exceed the price of a replacement, especially on the machines in the under-$200 tier where labour costs more than the appliance. Scale is the main killer of home espresso machines, and unlike most failures it is entirely preventable.

How often: your water decides, not the calendar

Descaling frequency is a function of water hardness and how much water you put through the machine. Hardness is usually expressed in milligrams per litre as calcium carbonate: below about 60 mg/L is soft, 60–120 is moderately hard, 120–180 is hard, and above that is very hard. Your municipal water utility publishes the number, and a $10 packet of test strips settles it for well water.

As a starting frame, manufacturers commonly specify something in the range of every three to six months on soft water, every two to three months on moderately hard water, and monthly to every six weeks on hard water — then adjust for volume, since a household pulling six drinks a day pushes several times the water of one pulling a single morning shot. Many machines also count for you and simply ask. If yours has a descale indicator, believe it: it's tracking volume, which is a better proxy than your memory. Whatever your manual specifies for your model overrides these general ranges.

The honest instruction: use your manufacturer's procedure and product

We're deliberately not giving you a recipe. Use the descaling product your machine's manufacturer specifies, and follow their procedure for your model. That isn't a legal hedge, it's mechanical: the dwell time, dilution and number of rinse cycles in a manual are matched to that machine's metals, seals and pump, and some warranties are conditional on using an approved descaler. Machines differ in ways that matter — where the solution needs to travel, whether the steam circuit is included, whether the pump can be run dry, and whether there's a hidden reservoir that keeps holding solution after the tank looks empty.

Which is also why we'd avoid the vinegar recipes that dominate search results. Acetic acid is variable in strength, it's harsher on some seals and on aluminum than the citric- and lactic-acid blends sold as descalers, and its smell has a talent for surviving in a boiler for weeks. If your manual names vinegar as acceptable for your model, fine. If it doesn't, don't improvise with your own machine as the experiment.

Descaler is not detergent — they solve opposite problems

This confusion damages machines. Descaler is acidic and dissolves mineral scale; it goes in the water tank and travels the water path — boiler, thermoblock, brew and steam circuits. Group detergent is alkaline and dissolves coffee oils; it goes in the blind disc for a backflush, or in a bowl for soaking baskets and screens. Swapping them accomplishes nothing useful in either direction: detergent in the tank won't remove a milligram of scale, and acid in a blind-disc backflush won't shift oxidized oil while putting acid exactly where it isn't wanted. Buy both, keep them separate, and don't ever mix them in one operation.

Why you must finish the rinse

The step people skip is the one that protects the machine. Descaling solution keeps reacting as long as it's in contact with metal and rubber, and it doesn't distinguish between limescale and your gaskets, brass fittings or aluminum boiler. Acid left sitting inside can pit metal, harden seals prematurely, and taint the next dozen shots with a sour, chemical edge. So: run the full number of clean-water tanks the manual specifies — often two or three complete tanks, through both the brew path and the steam wand — and don't leave a machine standing full of solution overnight unless the manual explicitly prescribes a soak. On the same logic, dissolve powdered descaler completely in warm water before it goes in the tank. Undissolved granules don't descale anything; they sit in the tank, get drawn into the pump, and act as an abrasive.

Built-in programs versus a manual cycle

Some machines run the cycle for you, which removes most of the ways to get it wrong. The AMZCHEF Espresso Machine with Grinder lists a descaling function, so the machine controls the pump timing and dwell itself; you supply the solution and follow the prompts. Super-automatics like the De'Longhi Magnifica Start are built around this idea, and the Ninja CFN601's descaling routine is effectively its main maintenance task, since a capsule machine has no group to clean.

If your machine has no program, the manual cycle generally looks like the steps below — but treat this as the shape of the job, not as a substitute for your manual's exact instructions.

  1. Turn the machine off and let it cool. Empty the drip tray. Remove the water filter if one is fitted.
  2. Dissolve the manufacturer's descaler fully in the volume of water their instructions specify, and fill the tank with it.
  3. Place a large container under the group and the steam wand. The output is hot, and there will be more of it than you expect.
  4. Run solution through the brew path in stages, and also through the steam wand and hot-water outlet if the manual includes them — scale forms everywhere water gets hot, and the steam circuit is the part people forget.
  5. Pause where the manual tells you to pause. Dwell time is how a descaler works; rushing it means you run the whole cycle for half the effect.
  6. Rinse with two to three full tanks of clean water, through every path you sent solution down, until there's no taste or smell of the product.
  7. Refit a fresh water filter, then pull and discard one shot before you drink anything from the machine.

On handling, briefly and practically: descalers are acids, so work in a ventilated kitchen, keep the solution off your skin and out of your eyes, don't lean over the outlet while hot liquid is running, keep it away from children and pets, and read the product label alongside your manual. General guidance, not a substitute for either document.

Upstream

Fix the water and you halve the problem

Descaling removes scale. Better water stops making it. If you use filtered water or a low-mineral bottled water instead of hard tap water, there is simply less calcium and magnesium going in, so less comes out on the element. The arithmetic is crude but useful: if switching sources takes you from hard water to soft, and your manual's interval scales with hardness the way most do, you can reasonably expect to descale roughly half as often. That assumes the same number of drinks per day and no change in tank volume.

A basic carbon or ion-exchange pitcher filter is enough for most people, as is bottled spring water with a low mineral content printed on the label. Two cautions. Water from a salt-based softener isn't a great choice for coffee: the sodium that replaces the calcium doesn't build scale, but it can be corrosive over time and it tastes flat in the cup. And if your machine came with its own in-tank filter, use it — it's cheaper than a pitcher and it's matched to the machine.

Don't reach for distilled water

Zero-TDS water — distilled, or output from a de-ionizing filter — looks like the perfect answer and is usually a mistake. Two reasons.

The practical one: many machines detect water level or presence electrically, using a probe that relies on the water conducting a small current between contacts. Pure water is a poor conductor, so a machine using that method can read a full tank as empty, refuse to pump, or throw a fill error. It looks like a fault and isn't one. Super-automatics and machines with electronic level sensing are the usual victims.

The taste one: extraction needs some mineral content. Brewing standards put the useful range for total dissolved solids somewhere around 75 to 150 mg/L, with roughly 150 as a common target, because those minerals participate in pulling flavour compounds out of the coffee. Water with nothing in it makes espresso that tastes thin and dull. Very pure water is also more aggressive toward some metals than moderately mineralized water. Aim for the middle: filtered, not stripped.

Cleaning first, dialing second

If a machine that used to make good espresso stopped, clean it before you change anything else. Adjusting grind to compensate for a clogged basket or a leaking gasket just buries the fault under a second variable, and you'll spend a week chasing a shot that was never about the coffee.

FAQ

Common questions

How often should I descale my espresso machine?

Water hardness sets the interval, not the calendar. As a general frame, manufacturers commonly specify every three to six months on soft water (below about 60 mg/L as calcium carbonate), every two to three months on moderately hard water (60–120), and monthly to six-weekly on hard water (above 120) — adjusted upward for volume, since a household making six drinks a day pushes several times the water of one making a single morning shot. Your manual's interval for your specific model takes precedence, and if the machine has a descale indicator, trust it, because it's counting volume rather than relying on your memory.

Can I use vinegar to descale an espresso machine?

We'd avoid it unless your manual names it as acceptable for your model. Acetic acid varies in strength, it's harsher on some seals and on aluminum than the citric- and lactic-acid blends sold specifically as descalers, and the smell can linger in a boiler for weeks. The safer answer is also the simplest: use the descaling product your manufacturer specifies and follow their procedure, because the dilution, dwell time and rinse count in the manual are matched to your machine's metals and seals. Whatever you use, dissolve it fully and finish every rinse tank — acid left inside keeps working on gaskets and metal.

Can I backflush any espresso machine?

No, and trying can do harm. Backflushing requires a three-way solenoid valve, the part that dumps the pressure above the puck to the drip tray at the end of a shot and gives you a dry puck. With that valve, a blind disc in the portafilter forces water back through the shower screen and out the drain, carrying oils with it. Without it there's no drain path, so the pump just strains against a dead end. Check your manual for the word "backflush" or for a blind or cleaning disc in the box contents — many entry-level machines have neither and should be cleaned by soaking the basket, brushing the screen and flushing plain water.

Is distilled water good for an espresso machine?

No, on two counts. Many machines sense water level electrically, using the water's conductivity to complete a circuit; distilled or de-ionized water conducts poorly, so the machine can read a full tank as empty and refuse to pump. And extraction needs some mineral content — brewing standards put the useful total-dissolved-solids range at roughly 75 to 150 mg/L — so zero-mineral water makes flat, thin-tasting espresso. Use filtered or low-mineral bottled water instead: enough minerals to taste right, few enough to slow scale formation.

What's the difference between descaler and espresso machine cleaner?

They target opposite residues with opposite chemistry. Descaler is acidic and dissolves mineral limescale; it goes in the water tank and travels the water path, including the steam circuit. Espresso machine cleaner, the detergent used for backflushing and soaking, is alkaline and dissolves coffee oils; it goes in a blind disc or a bowl and never in the tank. Neither substitutes for the other, and you shouldn't combine them in one operation. You need both products, kept separate, on different schedules.
