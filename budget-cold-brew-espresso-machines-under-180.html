What the cold brew setting actually changes

Espresso and cold brew are not the same drink forced through different settings on a dial. They are two different extraction methods, and the difference is pressure and heat, not just temperature.

A standard espresso shot pushes near-boiling water through a tightly packed, finely ground puck at roughly 9 bar of pressure for 25 to 30 seconds. The heat and the pressure do the work together: hot water dissolves and carries flavor compounds fast, and the pressure forces it through a bed of coffee that would otherwise barely let water pass. That is also why the heating element matters so much to shot quality — a boiler or thermoblock that swings in temperature changes the shot it pulls, which is the whole subject of our thermoblock vs. boiler guide.

Traditional cold brew throws both of those levers away on purpose. Coarsely ground coffee sits in room-temperature or cold water for 12 to 24 hours with no pressure at all, and time replaces heat and force as the thing doing the extracting. The result is a concentrate, typically diluted before drinking, that comes out less acidic and less bitter than hot-brewed coffee — the compounds behind sharp acidity and bitterness extract more readily at high temperature than cold ones do, a basic fact about coffee chemistry, not a claim about any one machine.

What a "cold brew setting" on one of these three machines changes is the question of how much of that slow steep you actually have to do yourself. None of the three listings here publish the internal mechanics of their cold brew cycle — no cycle length in minutes, no stated water temperature, no description of whether the pump recirculates water through the grounds or simply runs a slower, unpressurized pour. That is a real gap in the public spec sheet, and rather than guess at engineering none of these manufacturers have disclosed, we are telling you plainly: what we know for certain is what you select on the machine, not the engineering behind it. A cold brew mode on a machine like these is built to shortcut the traditional overnight steep into something you start with a button rather than a pitcher in the refrigerator — convenience is the pitch, not a claim that it replicates a 24-hour steep exactly.

PID and a touchscreen, for $104.99

Cold brew does not involve the machine's heating element at all — it is, by definition, made without heat. So when the $104.99 machine's listing leads with "PID Control and Easy Touch Operation" right alongside cold brew, it is worth separating what each of those actually improves, because the PID control is not doing anything for the cold brew cycle.

A PID, short for proportional-integral-derivative controller, is a well-understood piece of control electronics used across home espresso machines to hold a heating element's temperature inside a tight band rather than letting it swing the way a basic on/off thermostat does. The reason that matters is specific to the hot side: an espresso shot is sensitive to water temperature by only a few degrees, and a boiler or thermoblock that overshoots and undershoots on every cycle pulls a noticeably less consistent shot than one a PID is holding steady. Our guide to thermoblock vs. boiler machines goes deeper on why that temperature stability is the thing that actually separates a good cheap machine from a mediocre one.

So what the $104.99 machine's PID and touchscreen add beyond the cold brew mode is a better, steadier hot shot and an easier way to select between its modes digitally rather than with a row of physical dials. The value proposition here is really two machines in one listing: a hot-side upgrade (PID) that does nothing for cold brew, and a cold brew mode that does nothing for the hot side. Buying this machine for the touchscreen alone, expecting it to also make the cold brew taste different, is the wrong read of what each feature does.

None of the three machines in this guide include a built-in scale to weigh the dose going into the hot shot, which is a separate upgrade some pricier machines offer — see our guide to espresso machines with a built-in scale if gram-accurate dosing on the hot side matters more to you than the cold brew function does.

Making cold brew without a dedicated setting

None of this requires a special machine at all, and it is worth saying plainly before you spend anything: traditional cold brew is a steeping method, not an espresso technique, so any espresso machine's absence of a cold brew button does not actually stop you from making it.

The standard home method needs four things, none of which are specific to any brand here. Coarsely ground coffee — roughly the texture used for a French press, because fine espresso grounds turn to sludge over a long steep and clog a filter. Cold or room-temperature water, at a ratio commonly started around 1 part coffee to 4 parts water by weight for a concentrate meant to be diluted before drinking. A steeping vessel with some kind of filter — a French press, a large jar with a cheesecloth pour, or a dedicated cold brew carafe with a built-in mesh filter all work on the same principle. And time: 12 to 24 hours in the refrigerator, with longer generally producing a stronger, slightly more bitter concentrate and shorter producing something milder.

After the steep, strain the grounds out and dilute the concentrate with water or milk before serving over ice — straight concentrate is usually too strong to drink undiluted. The process asks for a coarse grind, a filter, a fridge and patience; no machine is strictly required to make a legitimate batch. What a dedicated cold brew setting buys you is skipping the overnight wait, which is a real convenience — just not a different drink than the one you can make for free with gear you probably already own.

One thing not to assume

A "cold brew setting" and a 24-hour steeped cold brew are not guaranteed to taste identical, because none of these three manufacturers has published exactly how their on-demand cycle works. Treat the setting as a faster, more convenient version of the same idea rather than an exact substitute, and judge the result by taste rather than by the name on the button.

Frequently asked questions

Is the cold brew made in these machines the same as a 24-hour steeped cold brew?

Not necessarily identical. Traditional cold brew steeps coarse grounds in cold water for 12 to 24 hours with no pressure or heat. None of these three machines publishes the internal mechanics of its cold brew cycle, so we cannot confirm the cycle time or method matches that traditional steep exactly. Treat the setting as a faster, more convenient version of the idea rather than a guaranteed substitute.

Do I need a grinder to use the cold brew setting on these machines?

Only the $179.99 Espresso Machine with Grinder & Steam Milk Frother includes a built-in grinder. The VIOMI T1A at $99.99 and the $104.99 touchscreen model do not list one, so you would use pre-ground coffee or add a separate grinder with either of those two.

What does PID control actually add if cold brew doesn't use heat?

PID control holds the heating element's temperature in a tight band, which improves the consistency of hot espresso shots specifically. It does nothing for the cold brew cycle, since cold brew is made without heat by definition. On the $104.99 machine, the PID and touchscreen are a hot-side upgrade bundled with, not built into, the cold brew function.

Can I make cold brew in an espresso machine that has no cold brew button?

Yes. Traditional cold brew is a steeping method, not something unique to these machines: coarsely ground coffee steeped in cold water for 12 to 24 hours, strained through a filter, then diluted to taste. A French press, a jar with cheesecloth, or a dedicated cold brew carafe all work. A dedicated setting saves the wait and the separate equipment; it is not the only way to make the drink.
