What a cold brew setting actually does versus steeping grounds overnight

Traditional cold brew is a patience method. Coarse grounds sit in room-temperature or cold water for 12 to 24 hours, and because there's no heat to accelerate extraction, the process pulls out different compounds than hot brewing does — less of the acid and bitterness that heat extracts quickly, more of the sweeter, rounder notes that take time to dissolve. The trade is time for a smoother, lower-acid cup, and it's why a pitcher of cold brew in the fridge is a batch project, not a single-serve one.

A machine's "cold brew mode" is an attempt to compress that overnight process into a button press, and the honest way to think about it is as a shortcut, not a replacement. Some machines do this by chilling the water going into the group head and running a longer, gentler cycle than a hot shot — still measured in a minute or two, not hours. Others push cold or cool water through the grounds under the same kind of pressure used for espresso, which extracts fast regardless of water temperature, producing something people call "cold brew" that is really a cold-pressed espresso concentrate. Neither is the 12-hour steep, and a machine that claims to replicate true cold brew in under a minute is compressing a chemistry-dependent process into a fraction of its usual time — worth knowing before you expect an identical cup.

What a true steeping mode changes, by contrast, is the machine's job: instead of forcing water through the puck the way it does for a hot shot, it has to hold grounds and water together for an extended period and then separate them, which is mechanically a different task from pulling espresso. That's the feature to look for if overnight-style cold brew, rather than a cold espresso variant, is what you're after.

The three approaches in this catalogue

Across the machines we cover on this site, "cold brew" resolves into three distinct mechanisms. Knowing which one a listing means before you buy saves a returned package.

01

Cold-pressed espresso

Cold or cool water forced through grounds at espresso-like pressure. Fast — closer to a minute than to hours — and the result is a concentrate meant to go over ice, not a steeped brew.

02

Dedicated cold temperature mode

A machine setting that runs the water at a lower temperature and a different cycle than its hot-brew mode, without necessarily changing the pressure mechanism underneath.

03

True steeped cold brew

Grounds and cold water held together for an extended steep, then separated — the closest a machine gets to overnight-pitcher cold brew, and mechanically the most different from pulling a hot shot.

The GeniCup and the AIRMSEN both list a cold-brew capability alongside a 20-bar pump and a built-in grinder, which is the profile of the first approach: a pressure-driven cold shot rather than a steep. The Ninja Luxe Café Premier is explicitly built as a 3-in-1 — espresso, drip and cold brew as three separate modes rather than one machine straining to do everything through the same espresso group head — which is the structural sign of the third approach. We don't have a manufacturer breakdown of exactly how each cycle times or chills its water beyond what's in the listings, so where a mechanism isn't spelled out, we say so rather than guess.

Iced americano vs cold brew vs cold-pressed espresso: what each tastes like

These three drinks get used interchangeably in casual conversation and they shouldn't be, because the extraction method behind each one changes what lands in the glass.

An iced Americano starts as a normal hot espresso shot — pulled at roughly 9 bar of pressure the way any espresso is — poured or shot directly over ice. The heat-driven extraction pulls the same brightness and body a hot shot would; the ice just chills and dilutes it afterward. That means it carries more of the acidity and immediacy that heat extraction produces, softened by dilution rather than avoided.

Cold brew, the steeped version, never sees heat. Coarse grounds sit in cold water for hours, and the slow extraction favors different compounds than a fast hot pull does — typically read as smoother and less acidic, with a heavier, more syrup-like body once it's concentrated. It's also usually served over ice as a concentrate diluted with water or milk, similar in serving style to an Americano but arrived at by a completely different process.

Cold-pressed espresso — what the GeniCup and AIRMSEN cold-brew settings most likely produce — splits the difference mechanically: cold or cool water forced through grounds under pressure, in roughly the same timeframe as a hot shot rather than over hours. Because pressure rather than heat is doing the extracting, the result tends to land between the two: less of the sharp acidity that heat pulls fast, but without the hours-long mellowing of a true steep. It's a real style of its own, not a lesser version of either — but it is worth knowing that's what you're getting when a machine markets it simply as "cold brew."

None of this is a claim we can rate or score — we have no lab and no panel of tasters, just the mechanics of what each method does to water and coffee. Cleaning and switching back to hot brewing

A cold-brew setting adds a cleaning step that a hot-only machine doesn't have, and it's worth planning for before the first use rather than discovering it a week in.

On the two cold-pressed-espresso machines here — the GeniCup and the AIRMSEN — the water path for a cold shot is the same group head and lines used for hot espresso, so the cleaning routine doesn't fundamentally change: flush the group head, wipe the portafilter, and follow the manufacturer's descaling schedule based on your water hardness, same as any pump machine. The one thing worth double-checking on the current listing is whether the machine needs to fully cool down between a cold cycle and a hot one, or whether it can switch immediately — that detail isn't specified in what we have, so treat it as a question for the product page or manual rather than an assumption.

On a true steeping machine like the Ninja Luxe Café Premier, the cold-brew path is more separate from the espresso and drip paths, so there's more to keep clean: a steeping basket or reservoir that held wet grounds for an extended period needs to be emptied and rinsed promptly, since spent grounds left sitting are the kind of thing that encourages buildup and odor faster than a quick espresso puck would. Manufacturer cleaning instructions for the cold-brew basket specifically are the reference to follow rather than treating it like the espresso side of the machine.

Across all of them, limescale from tap water is still the long-term threat to any heating element or pump, cold-brew feature or not — our guide to what an espresso machine actually costs to run covers descaling frequency and supplies as part of the ongoing cost, separate from the machine's purchase price.

Who should just make iced espresso the simple way instead

A dedicated cold-brew button is solving a real problem for some people and adding an unnecessary decision for others, and it's worth being honest about which camp you're in before paying extra for the feature.

If what you actually want most mornings is an iced latte or an iced Americano, the simplest and cheapest path is a machine that pulls a good hot shot and a glass of ice — no cold-brew mode required. Pull the shot, pour it over ice, add milk if you want it; the dilution from the ice does most of the work a "cold" setting is trying to replicate anyway. Any semi-automatic with a burr grinder does this job, including the XIXUBX above, whose PID control is arguably more useful for this than a cold-brew button would be, since dialing in a slightly hotter or more concentrated shot before it hits ice is a real lever a fixed cold cycle doesn't give you.

Where a dedicated cold-brew feature earns its keep is if you specifically want the steeped style — smoother, less acidic, and genuinely different in character from an iced hot shot — without setting up a separate cold-brew pitcher and waiting overnight. That's a real use case, and it's the one the Ninja Luxe Café Premier is built for. It's a much weaker case for the cold-pressed-espresso machines here, where the honest comparison is: you're paying for a labeled "cold brew" button that produces something closer to a fast cold shot you could approximate by pulling espresso and pouring it over ice anyway. Frequently asked questions

Is a machine's cold brew mode the same as steeping grounds overnight?

Usually not. Traditional cold brew steeps coarse grounds in cold water for 12 to 24 hours with no heat and no pressure. Most machine "cold brew" settings that share a group head with the espresso function are pressure-driven cold-pressed espresso instead — fast, concentrated, and mechanically closer to a cold espresso shot than to an overnight steep. A machine like the Ninja Luxe Café Premier, which runs cold brew as a separate mode from its espresso and drip functions, is closer to the true steeped style.

What's the difference between an iced Americano and cold brew?

An iced Americano is a hot-extracted espresso shot poured over ice, so it carries the acidity and brightness that heat pulls out quickly, softened by dilution. Cold brew is steeped for hours with no heat, which favors different, generally smoother and less acidic compounds, and produces a heavier-bodied concentrate that's also usually diluted before drinking. Same serving style over ice, different extraction chemistry underneath.

Do I need to clean a cold-brew machine differently than a hot-only one?

If the cold-brew feature shares the espresso group head, as it does on pressure-driven machines like the GeniCup and AIRMSEN, the cleaning routine is largely the same as any pump espresso machine: flush the group, wipe the portafilter, descale on schedule. Machines with a separate steeping basket, like the Ninja Luxe Café Premier's cold-brew mode, need that basket emptied and rinsed promptly since wet spent grounds left sitting encourage buildup faster than a quick espresso puck.

Should I buy a machine with a cold-brew button, or just pour hot espresso over ice?

If you want an iced latte or Americano, pulling a normal hot shot over ice does the job without paying for an extra setting — any semi-automatic with a burr grinder handles this, and a PID-equipped machine like the XIXUBX arguably gives you more useful control over the shot going into the ice than a fixed cold cycle would. A dedicated cold-brew feature is worth paying for specifically if you want the smoother, steeped style of cold brew without setting up a separate pitcher overnight.
